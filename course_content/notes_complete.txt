                 Introduction to Thermodynamics

    · Thermodynamics is the foundation of Material Science, explaining the
       behaviour of matter and the interactions between Materials, heat, and
       work.

    · Thermodynamics is one of the basic sciences, which mathematically and
       quantitatively deals with heat, work, transfer of materials in equilibrium,
       material transitions, and their relationships with properties of materials.

    · Two aspects of Thermodynamics will be covered in this course:

           Classical Thermodynamics

1. First and Second Law of Thermodynamics
2. Free Energy
3. Equilibrium

         Thermodynamics of materials

1. Thermodynamics of Mixtures
2. Engineering solid solutions

    · Thermodynamics consists of four essential laws that govern the study of
       energetic transitions and the relationships between thermodynamic
       properties.

               Applications of thermodynamics

· Below are some examples in how Thermodynamics can be applied in the
    world we live in.

                             Heat engines

· According to the Second Law, heat always flows from a body of higher
    temperature to one of lower temperature. This applies to all types of heat
    engines, including Otto and Diesel engines, and for all types of working fluids
    used in the engines. This law led to the progress of present-day vehicles.

           Why do we sweat in a crowded room?

· As the room heats up, our body starts to produce sweat. Heat is transferred to
    the sweat, which then evaporates, cooling us down. This process adds heat
    to the room and occurs due to the First and Second Law of thermodynamics.

· This example demonstrates how heat is not lost but instead transferred,
    attaining equilibrium with maximum entropy.

                        The concept of work

· Work is the product of the amount of force that causes the motion and the
    distance through which the force acts.

· The two essential factors required for performing work (Nm or J) are a force
    and a displacement parallel to the force.

· Force and displacement are vector quantities and dependent on direction.

             Mechanical Work ­ Kinetic Energy KE

· Kinetic energy is a form of energy that an object or a particle has by
   reason of its motion.

· When work, which transfers energy, is done on an object by applying
   a net force, the object speeds up and gains Kinetic energy.

            Types of Kinetic Energy

               one place to another place.

Rotational     Rotational kinetic energy is from an
               object's rotational motion about an axis.

Vibrational    Vibrational kinetic energy is from an
               object's vibrational motion.

· Kinetic energy is a property of a moving object or particle and is
   dependent on its mass and speed. Hence its equation is,

      Kinetic energy = ½ mv2

· Kinetic energy (J or Kg.m/s2)
   m = mass (Kg)
   v = velocity (m/s2)

· Kinetic Energy can be derived from other forms of energy, for
   example, potential energy.

· Consider a bow being drawn back to fire an arrow. As the string is
   being stretched, it gains potential energy.

   Once the string releases, all the stored energy is transferred to the
   arrow, generating a net force opposite to the direction which it was
   drawn, causing it to be shot outward.

· To find the KE from this, find the weight of the arrow (m), and
   measure the speed at which the arrow left the bow (v). Substitute
   these values into the formula to find the KE.

· To find PE from this, calculate the length the bow was drawn back (h)
   and find the weight of the arrow (m). Substitute these values into the
   formula to find PE.

                           Internal Energy (U)

· One other type of energy is called internal energy, denoted by U.
· It is the sum of all microscopic forms of energy of a system and may

   be viewed as the sum of all the kinetic and potential energy.
MS1016 - Thermodynamics of Material  Chapter 1

· It includes the energies of all the chemical bonds and free conduction
   electrons in metals.

· Internal energy is dependent on the inherent qualities and properties
   of the materials of the system. For example, their composition,
   physical form and environmental variables like pressure, temperature,
   etc.

· Internal energy has many forms, including mechanical, chemical,
   electrical, magnetic, surface, thermal and more.

· Internal energy will increase when the temperature increases.

                    State of a system

· The state of a thermodynamic system at any instant is its condition of
   existence at that instant specified by values of a certain number of
   state variables.

· Different properties can be used to describe the state of a system.

· These include comprise Energy, Entropy, Chemical composition,
   Temperature, Pressure, Volume, External field, and Substance size.

· The specification of the state of the system must include the values of
   these properties.

· A state of the system, which can be reproduced, means that the state
   is well defined.
MS1016 - Thermodynamics of Material  Chapter 1

              Property versus Quantity

· A property of a system depends only on the state of the system, not
   how that state was obtained.

· This means that path dependent functions, namely work and heat,
   cannot be defined as properties, but are instead called quantities.

· Therefore, it is incorrect to say a system contains an amount of heat
   or work in it.

· Rather the system does an amount of work or transfers an amount of
   heat.

Nature of thermodynamic properties

Intensive  Independent of the amount of matter in the
           system

           Can be specified at a specific point in the
           system, such as pressure, temperature, or
           specific volume.

           Dependent on the amount of matter in the
MS1016 - Thermodynamics of Material  Chapter 1

                           system.

Extensive  Cannot be specified at a particular point in
           space.

           Its value for the entire system is equal to
           the sum of its values for all parts of the
           system, such as volume, energy, and
           mass.

· The ratio between two extensive properties is an intensive property.

                Thermodynamic Equilibrium state

· If a system is undisturbed for a period, it will settle down until no
   further change occurs macroscopically. At this point, the system is
   said to be in equilibrium.

                      Unbalanced potential

· Unbalanced potential is the driving force that leads to a change of
   state. A system in equilibrium does not have unbalanced potential.

· This unbalanced potential may be mechanical, thermal, chemical, or
   any combination of them.
MS1016 - Thermodynamics of Material  Chapter 1

                     Thermal equilibrium

· If a temperature gradient exists, heat transfer will take place.

· Heat transfer will continuously take place until everything in the
   system is the same temperature, at which the system is in a state of
   thermal equilibrium.

                   Mechanical equilibrium

· If there are variations in pressure or elastic stress within the system,
   parts of the system may move, either expanding or contracting.

· Eventually, these motions will cease, at which point the system is in
   mechanical equilibrium.

                     Chemical equilibrium

· If a system has no tendency to undergo either a chemical reaction or
   process such as diffusion or solution, the system is regarded to be in
   a state of chemical equilibrium.

· Once ALL the various forms of equilibriums are satisfied, a system is
   said to be in a state of thermodynamic equilibrium.

                  State and Non-state Functions

· Thermodynamic functions such as pressure, volume, heat, work, etc,
   may be categorised into state and non-state functions.
MS1016 - Thermodynamics of Material  Chapter 1

· State functions are NOT path dependent, meaning they are ONLY

                            2. State functions

· A state function is a thermodynamic function that is independent of the path
    between 2 equilibrium states. The change in internal energy is defined by the
    final and initial equilibrium states of that system. The intermediate energy
    states can be ignored.

· A state function in thermodynamics is a property that only depends on the
    current state of a system, regardless of how it got to that state. It's like a
    snapshot of the system at a particular moment.
    Let's imagine you have a toy car. The state of the car can be described by its
    color, size, and shape. These properties don't change no matter how the car
    got to that state. For example, if you painted the car red, it will stay red until
    you paint it a different color.
    In thermodynamics, we have similar properties for substances, like
    temperature, pressure, and volume. These properties only depend on the
    current state of the substance, not how it got there. So, if you heat up a
    substance, its temperature will increase, and if you cool it down, its
    temperature will decrease. But once you stop heating or cooling it, the
    temperature will stay the same until you change it again.
    State functions are important because they help us understand and predict
    how substances behave in different conditions.

· Hence the change in internal energy, U is simply,

                U = U2 ­ U1

· where 1 and 2 are the initial and final states respectively. All we need then is
    all the parameters describing the final and initial states, such as pressure,
    temperature, etc.
MS1016 - Thermodynamics of Material  Chapter 2

· In thermodynamics, work done refers to quasi-static (reversible) changes only.

· A process is reversible when the system's initial state can be restored with no

3. The First law of Thermodynamics states:

   "A system that performs an amount of work, (W) on its surroundings and
   receives an amount of heat, (Q) is defined to have undergone
   a change in internal energy, U."

 Or simply, U = Q ­ W

· The First Law may be written in a differential form by simply differentiating it.

                 dU = Q + W

· The law is a general equation that can be applied to any field, including
    electrical, magnetic, and chemical. For completeness, the potential energy
    (PE) and kinetic energy (KE) may be added into the general equation.

   Q + W = dU + d(PE) + d(KE)

· Ultimately the First Law is simply the conservation of energy as it shows how
    thermal energy in a system cannot be created or destroyed, only changed
    from one form to another.

                                     4. Enthalpy

· All natural processes, chemical reactions and industrial processes take place
    at constant pressure (i.e., atmospheric pressure). In most cases it is also
    associated with volume changes (ice melting to water, water boiling to steam).
    If a process takes place with a volume change, the system itself must do work.

          Recall the equation to calculate work done, W =  P dV

· If the pressure is constant, work done W = PV where V is the volume
    change. If pressure is constant, then it is more convenient to use a new
    thermodynamic function of the macrostate called enthalpy (H).

  Enthalpy replaces heat when pressure is kept constant.
                             It is defined as:

                H = U + PV

· The unit for enthalpy is Joules (J).
· U, V and H are extensive (dependent on mass of system)
· U, P, V and H are all functions of the macrostate.
· Enthalpy is a state function.

                Specific enthalpy

         is the enthalpy per mass and is given as,

               H = U + PV

             6. Adiabatic Flow and Compression

· Adiabatic processes are defined by processes in which no heat is added or
    removed from the system.

· Consider a fluid that flows through a valve (the system) adiabatically. Assume
    that the valve is insulated, and no heat can flow into or out of the system. Also
    assume that the process is in a steady state.

                              Figure 1.15 Flow through a valve

· An adiabatic system assumes no heat flows in or out of the system.

   Therefore, Q = 0.

                                   Microstates

· The previous chapter introduced the concept of a system's Macrostate, which
    may be defined by properties such as temperature (T), pressure (P), volume (V),
    etc.

· Conversely, a system's Microstate is defined by the molecular positions and
    velocities of all constituent particles of the system. However, due to the number of
    particles in a system (1023), experimentally, this is an impossible task.

             However, consider a system in steady state or equilibrium.
· The system's microstate rapidly changes with time, yet its macrostate is constant,

    implying that a huge number of microstates correspond to a single macrostate!

                      Accessible Microstates

· There always exists some constraints limiting the number of microstates of a
    system, for example available volume and total energy.

· Accessible microstates are microstates that correspond to both these constraints

   and the given macrostate and is often denoted by .

· These constraints may be relaxed, for example by increasing the volume or energy
    of the system, which will affect the number of accessible microstates the system
    will have.

                           Consider two different closed systems.

Figure 2: Example of constraints imposed on a system limiting the number of microstates that
                               can be accessed, hence accessible microstates.

The following statements associated with the microstates are true for
                  processes occurring in an isolated system.

  1. Spontaneous processes will always result in an increase in the number of
       accessible microstates, .

            · Increasing the system's temperature increases the number of microstates
                 as the increases in kinetic energy of the molecules results in greater
                 motion.

            · Increasing the number of molecules in a system increases the number of
                 microstates as there are more possible arrangements the molecules may
                 adopt.
MS1016 - Thermodynamics of Material  Chapter 2

· Increasing the volume of a system increases the number of potential positions
    each molecule may be, increasing the number of microstates.

· Therefore, any change that results in a higher temperature, more molecules, or a
    larger volume yields an increase in the number of microstates.

2. Processes decreasing the number of accessible microstates of a system can
    only occur if the system interacts with another system or the surrounding.
    However, the change must result in an increase in the number of microstates for
    the combined system.

 3. The number of microstates is a maximum for an isolated system in equilibrium.

· In chapter 1 and 2, we learned how to measure the change in enthalpy and
    entropy.

· Some reactions or processes are spontaneous because they lower their energy

   by giving off heat (H < 0)

· Some are spontaneous because they result in an increase in entropy (S > 0).

· When both driving forces are favourable, the reaction is spontaneous.
    When both driving forces are unfavourable, the reaction is not spontaneous.

· What happens when one of the driving forces are favourable while the other is
    unfavourable?

· A property called Gibbs free energy is used which is defined by a balance
    between these two driving forces.

                                  Metastability

· Metastability denotes an intermediate energetic state within a dynamical
    system other than the system's state of least energy and are present due to the
    existence of energy barriers.

· These energy barriers must be overcome before a metastable state can be
    transformed into one with lower free energy.

· Diamond is kinetically metastable because thermodynamically it should change
    to graphite. However, this change is so slow that it is practically undetectable in a
    human lifetime.

· This is due to a huge activation energy barrier (temperatures >4500 K) that must
    be overcome for diamond to change into graphite.

· All the energy is used to break numerous C-C bonds, essentially destroying the
    entire crystal lattice, and rebuilding it. Therefore, once diamond is formed, it
    cannot convert back to graphite under normal conditions because the energy
    barrier is too high.

The concept of equilibrium is fundamental to the study of thermodynamics. When two
or more phases are in equilibrium, there is no driving force for change and the phases
are stable. Firstly, in this chapter, we will learn the criteria for equilibrium in a single
component system. Then, we will discuss the phase transitions of pure substances by
considering the factors that determine the positions and shapes of the boundaries
between the regions on a phase diagram. Lastly, the effect of applied pressure on

B. The dependence of stability on the conditions

Phase stability is dependent on the conditions, such as temperature and pressure. For

C. Phase diagrams
One of the most succinct ways of presenting the physical changes of state that a
substance can undergo is in terms of its phase diagram. The phase diagram of a
substance shows the regions of pressure and temperature which its various phases
are thermodynamically stable. The lines separating the regions, which are called
phase boundaries, show the values of P and T at which two phases coexist in
equilibrium. Moreover, the area where a phase is stable is called the phase field.

                                     Fig 4.3 An example of a simple phase diagram

When three phases are in equilibrium, then it is obvious that there is only one
temperature and pressure where all three chemical potentials are equal. The point
where three phases coexist in equilibrium is called triple point. The triple point of a
pure substance is outside our control and it is one of the characteristics of the

                         6|Phase Equilibrium in Single Component System
MS1016 - Thermodynamics of Material                    Chapter 4

substance. For example, the triple point of water lies at 273.16 K and 611 Pa. In this
condition, three phases of water (ice, liquid water, and water vapor) coexist in
equilibrium. Therefore, the water freezes, melts, evaporates, and sublimes at the same
time.

Gibbs Phase Rule

One of the most elegant calculations of the whole of thermodynamics, J.W. Gibbs

deduced the Gibbs Phase Rule, which is a general relation between the variance or

the number of variables, F, the number of components, C, and the number of phases

at equilibrium, P, for a system of any composition, the relationship is as follows,

                  F=C-P+2                                       (6.27)

By Abraham `23
                                                             2
MS1016 - Thermodynamics of Material                                         Chapter 6

To make it easier to understand, let's consider a one-component system, C = 1, such
as pure water. Hence, F = 3 - P. When only one phase is present, P = 1, then F = 2.
This means that two variables can be varied independently without changing the
number of phases, such as P and T.

The response of equilibria to temperature is one of Le Chatelier's principles. It predicts
that a system at equilibrium will tend to shift in the endothermic direction if the
temperature is raised, for then energy is absorbed as heat. Conversely, an equilibrium
can be expected to shift in the exothermic direction if the temperature is lowered, for
then energy is released.

To better understand the principle, let's see this illustration.

                                     AB  H = +x

From the example, the enthalpy is positive. Thus, we need energy to convert A into B.
We can, therefore, write the reaction as,

                                              Energy + A  B

Since energy is related to temperature, if we increase the temperature, we can say the
energy increases. Using our previous illustration about our position (Q) and the
destination (K), if we increase the temperature, the reaction will move toward the right.
Conversely, if we decrease the temperature, the reaction will move toward the left.

The above illustration is for endothermic reactions. For exothermic reactions (negative
enthalpy), the energy will be on the right side.

                                              A  B + Energy

If we decrease the temperature, the energy will decrease. Hence, our position (Q) will
be on the left, so that the reaction will move toward the right. Conversely, if we increase
the temperature, the reaction will move toward the left.

Both of our illustrations are to assume that "Energy" as a component or compound.
Hence, we can put the "Energy" activity to the Q and K formula. However, this
assumption is false. The assumption is used to simplify our understanding of the
response of equilibria to temperature.
